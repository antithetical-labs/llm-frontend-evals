Acid design treats the interface as an active signal rather than a neutral container. It borrows from 1990s rave flyers, early web graphics, cyberpunk terminals, industrial labeling, glitch art, and the fluorescent language of warning systems. The result should feel synthetic, fast, slightly abrasive, and intentionally engineered—not generically “futuristic.” For Streamforge, that energy should be tempered by the precision of a professional development tool: expressive enough to feel live, but structured enough to scan during a technical broadcast.

**Palette:** Start with a near-black, cool-toned base—charcoal, ink, or deep blue-black—rather than pure black. Use one dominant acidic fluorescent such as electric chartreuse, toxic lime, or laser yellow-green for active states, stream indicators, prompts, and primary actions. Add a colder secondary signal color like ultraviolet, cobalt, or cyan, with occasional hot magenta or warning orange for forks, errors, and model events. Off-white should carry long-form text. Color is functional and concentrated: large dark fields interrupted by luminous bars, outlines, badges, and hard-edged highlights. Avoid soft gradients, tasteful pastel schemes, and evenly distributed rainbow color. If gradients appear, they should feel spectral, overexposed, or digitally banded.

**Typography:** Pair a confrontational display face—wide grotesk, compressed sans, or techno-industrial uppercase—with a highly legible neo-grotesk for interface copy and a monospace face for prompts, model output, repository metadata, timestamps, and code. Headlines can be oversized, tightly tracked, cropped, stacked, or interrupted by labels. Interface text should remain disciplined and relatively small, using uppercase, tabular numerals, and terse system language. The contrast between billboard-scale messaging and compact terminal notation creates the desired tension. Avoid friendly rounded SaaS typography, elegant editorial serifs, and excessive typographic variety.

**Texture and imagery:** Texture comes from the machinery of a live software session: code syntax, terminal grids, scanlines, cursor blocks, diff markings, waveform-like activity traces, pixel noise, compression artifacts, timestamps, and repository graphs. Use these as structural surfaces rather than decorative overlays. Thin grid lines, oversized crosshairs, registration marks, segmented borders, and subtle CRT grain can make the page feel transmitted instead of printed. Motion should suggest state changes—blinking live indicators, flowing event logs, prompt insertion, and branching fork diagrams—not ambient decoration. Avoid glassmorphism, glossy 3D blobs, generic AI sparkles, stock photographs of developers, and decorative glitch effects that impair reading.

**Layout logic:** The composition should resemble a broadcast console crossed with a developer workspace. Use a rigid modular grid, but allow one or two elements to violate it dramatically: an oversized headline, a vertical status rail, a ticker, or a stream preview that cuts across columns. Sections can be divided by hard rules and numbered like system modules. Dense metadata should sit beside generous dark negative space. Cards should feel like panes, feeds, or repository objects rather than rounded marketing tiles. Corners are mostly square or minimally clipped; borders are thin, bright, and explicit. Hierarchy is produced through scale, contrast, alignment, and signal color—not shadows and floating layers.

**Interaction language:** The page should communicate that everything is happening now. “LIVE,” viewer count, active model, branch name, build status, prompt count, and fork activity should be visible at a glance. Calls to action should sound operational—“Watch live,” “Start broadcasting,” “Fork repository”—and look like controls rather than lifestyle buttons. Hover and focus states can invert colors, reveal coordinates, shift labels, or trigger a short digital pulse. Accessibility still governs the system: fluorescent color cannot be the only state indicator, body text needs strong contrast, motion must be reducible, and code must remain genuinely readable.

**What it borrows:** Rave and club ephemera contribute fluorescent urgency and compressed typography; early web and terminal culture contribute visible systems, monospace notation, and imperfect digital texture; Swiss and modernist graphics contribute the underlying grid and information hierarchy; industrial interfaces contribute warning colors, labels, and status logic; cyberpunk contributes atmosphere, but not narrative clichés.

**What it rejects:** It rejects the frictionless, polite sameness of mainstream SaaS landing pages—soft blue-purple gradients, pill-shaped everything, floating dashboard mockups, vague “unlock your potential” copy, mascot illustrations, and excessive whitespace with little information. It also rejects illegibility for its own sake. The page can feel aggressive and experimental, but the product proposition, current streams, developer credibility, and fork action must remain immediate.

Overall, Streamforge should feel like entering a public build room at 02:13: code compiling, model responding, branches splitting, and an audience watching the whole process unfold. The aesthetic is acid, but the experience is exact.
